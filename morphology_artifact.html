Why the Robots Are Blobs

Every champion this project has evolved is an undifferentiated slab. The cause isn't the physics engine and it isn't a lack of compute — it's that a body is a level set of a smooth field, and the fitness function never asked for anything else.

01

The instrument

A robot's body is not a list of voxels. It's a network (a CPPN) that answers, for each of the 5832 grid positions, a single question: how strongly should there be material here? That produces a scalar landscape. The body is whatever stands above a waterline.

02

How a body is decoded

Four steps run every time a genome becomes a robot. Step three is where the shape is decided — and where the trouble starts.

STEP 1Query every position The network is evaluated at each grid point, taking its coordinates and distance from centre. 5832 queries
STEP 2Gate on presence Positions whose presence value clears zero become candidates. Roughly 43% of the grid does. 1766 candidates
STEP 3Keep the strongest Only the highest-confidence candidates survive, up to the voxel budget. This is the waterline. → 300 kept
STEP 4Drop loose parts Anything not face-connected to the main mass is deleted. Measured: this fires on 0–1% of bodies. It is not the constraint — limbs never form to begin with. 0–1% affected
The consequence

Because the surviving body is always the top slice of a smooth landscape, it is compact by construction. The waterline auto-adjusts to spend the entire budget, so thinning one region simply refills another. Until recently the budget was a fixed cap, which meant body size was not an evolvable trait at all — 98% of bodies came out at exactly the cap.

Not a sphere — a flattened slab, filling 41% of its own bounding box, with layer areas tapering 39 → 4 from base to top. A mound. There are no thin parts, no protrusions, and no functional regions: every voxel is the same material doing the same job. Legs would show the inverse profile, narrow at the bottom.

04

The papers did not get blobs

Cheney et al. (2013), the paper this project re-implements, evolved robots their authors named and catalogued: L-Walker, Incher, Push-Pull, Jitter, Jumper and Wings — described with hind legs, necks, heads and arms. They reached 3.5–4.0 body-lengths. Same encoding, same materials, same flat ground. The differences are in what evolution was asked for.

The synthesis

Blobs are not a physical inevitability. They are the optimal answer to the question this fitness function actually asks: travel forward, pay nothing for mass, pay nothing for muscle, fill the budget completely, and stay on the ground. A dense low slab wins that contest every time. The papers asked a different question and got animals.
